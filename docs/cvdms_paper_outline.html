1.1 Novelty positioning relative to Chen & Van Dyck 1997

Chen and Van Dyck[1] originally formulated the slice-transmission-operator (STO) matrix and derived the forward-scattering coefficient (FSC) and backscattering coefficient (BSC) operators that motivate the present work. Their treatment was analytical and did not provide adaptive truncation diagnostics, antialias control compatible with FFT/finite-difference Laplacians, or a flux-conserving Fresnel BSC suitable for routine TEM-scale grids. The novelty of the present manuscript is therefore twofold. Physically, we identify the splitting commutator $[\sigma V,\nabla_\perp^2/(4\pi K_0)]$ — sharply peaked at atomic columns and traced here through measurable channeling and HOLZ observables (Sec. 4.5) — as the operator that direct exponentiation retains and Lie–Trotter omits. Computationally, we convert the open-form Chen–Van Dyck scheme into a falsifiable framework with three pixel-resolved probes of this commutator: (i) the per-pixel divergence ratio $r$, monotone in the local scattering-to-diffraction ratio $\rho=\Delta z/\ell_\text{mfp}$; (ii) a stagnation count that detects regimes in which slice projection itself fails; and (iii) a Fresnel BSC operator with directly observable unitarity residual $|T|^2+|R|^2-1$. The framework re-positions Chen & Van Dyck's open scheme as a controlled diagnostic of when projected-slice multislice is physically valid, rather than as an alternative numerical recipe.

2. Abstract & Significance

2.1 Abstract (target ≤150 words)

Multiple elastic scattering of high-energy electrons in solids is conventionally simulated by Lie–Trotter splitting of the projected-slice scattering operator. The discarded commutator $[\sigma V,\nabla_\perp^2/(4\pi K_0)]$, sharply localized at atomic columns, controls channeling oscillations and HOLZ deficit lines but is not exposed during the simulation itself. Here we show that direct Taylor exponentiation of $\hat{K}=\sigma V+\nabla_\perp^2/(4\pi K_0)$, evaluated through a coefficient-scaled square-root cascade and tracked pixel by pixel, retains the commutator to all orders in $\Delta z$ and yields three measurable physical quantities — a divergence ratio $r$, a K-series stagnation count, and a Fresnel backscattering residual $|T|^2+|R|^2-1$ — that map locally onto the projected potential field $V(\mathbf{R})$. Across SrTiO$_3$, Si, and Au at 30–300 keV, validated against analytic limits, $\Delta z$-refined Fourier multislice, and a Bloch-wave channeling reference, the diagnostics define a specimen-aware operating envelope in the dimensionless control variables $\rho=\Delta z/\ell_\text{mfp}$ and $\eta=r_F/w_\text{col}$, and identify the commutator-sensitive regimes where slice-based propagation breaks down.

2.2 One-sentence significance statement (Comms Phys "Editor's summary")

A pixel-resolved series formulation of the projected-potential scattering operator captures the splitting commutator $[\sigma V,\nabla_\perp^2/(4\pi K_0)]$ that conventional Lie–Trotter multislice omits, turning the convergence of high-energy electron scattering into a directly measurable physical quantity and defining when slice-based simulations are physically controlled.

3. Introduction

~700–900 words. Open with the physics problem; show why current splitting-based multislice is uncontrolled in specific regimes; state the three measurable diagnostics introduced here; close with the four contributions.

3.1 The multiple-scattering problem at high energies

  • Fast electrons (30–300 keV) interact with crystals via cross-sections orders of magnitude larger than X-rays or neutrons; the Born approximation fails for all but the thinnest specimens[6].
  • The relativistically corrected Schrödinger-like equation $\nabla^2\Psi+(2\pi\hat{K})^2\Psi=0$ governs the propagation, with $\hat{K}=K_0\sqrt{1+\nabla_\perp^2/(2\pi K_0)^2+\sigma U/(\pi K_0)}$[1].
  • Practical simulation requires a slice decomposition; within each slice the potential is projected onto a transverse plane.
  • The conventional multislice scheme[6,7] approximates the slice exponential as $\exp(i\Delta z\,\hat{A})\exp(i\Delta z\,\hat{B})$ — a Lie–Trotter splitting whose commutator error is $\mathcal{O}(\Delta z^2[\hat{A},\hat{B}])$ and is not directly observable during the run.

3.2 Limits of the splitting approach

  • The neglected commutator can become large for strong potentials or thick slices: for 30 keV electrons ($\lambda\!\approx\!0.0698\,\text{Å}$) the per-slice scattering phase $\sigma V_\text{proj}\Delta z$ can exceed $\pi$ for heavy elements at $\Delta z>0.4$ Å, violating the thin-slice premise.
  • Earlier Taylor-series treatments[1,8] avoided splitting but lacked adaptive truncation; truncation was heuristic.
  • Existing high-accuracy alternatives — full multislice with refined slicing[2,3,4], Bloch-wave methods[6], and PRISM[3] — control accuracy through global parameters rather than per-pixel observables.

3.3 The potential field $V(\mathbf{R})$ as the source of the difficulty

The convergence problem is not abstract: it is a property of $V(\mathbf{R})$. Before introducing CVDMS we summarise the spatial and spectral structure of the projected potential that controls every diagnostic in the paper.
  • Spatial structure. The projected potential $V(\mathbf{R})=\sum_a V_a(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_a)$ peaks at atomic columns with $V_\text{peak}\!\propto\!Z^{4/3}$ and $1/e$ width $w_\text{col}\!\sim\!0.4$–$0.7$ Å; between columns it drops by 1–3 orders of magnitude[10]. Representative values from the Lobato parameterization[10] integrated over one unit-cell repeat with isotropic Debye–Waller factors $B_\text{Sr}=0.62$, $B_\text{Ti}=0.51$, $B_\text{O}=0.86$, $B_\text{Si}=0.46$, $B_\text{Au}=0.66$ Å$^2$ at 300 K[9]: SrTiO$_3$ [001] Sr column $V_\text{peak}\!\approx\!1060$ eV·Å; Si [110] column $\approx\!470$ eV·Å; Au [001] column $\approx\!2300$ eV·Å.
  • Spectral structure. The single-atom form factor $f_e(q)$ falls only as $q^{-2}$ at large $q$[10], so $\hat V(\mathbf{q})$ has heavy tails: high-$k$ amplitude is created in the very first $V\psi$ multiplication and grows by $\hat{V}*\hat{V}*\dots$ self-convolution at every K-iteration. This is why bandlimiting is a physical, not cosmetic, control.
  • Gradient structure. $\nabla_\perp V$ is sharply localised at atomic-column edges and drives transverse rescattering; $\partial_z V$ at slice boundaries drives backscattering (Sec. 6.6) and is what the Fresnel BSC operator integrates.
  • Thermal smearing. Frozen-phonon Debye–Waller factors $\exp(-B q^2/4)$ damp the high-$q$ tails of $\hat V$[9]; with the $B$ values quoted above this reduces $V_\text{peak}$ by 12–28 % and shifts the convergent/divergent boundary outward — i.e. thermal motion enlarges the operating envelope, a physically intuitive but quantitatively under-reported effect that we make explicit by reporting $V^{(0)}$ and $V^{(B)}$ as separate fields throughout (Sec. 6.10).

3.5 The CVDMS approach

  • The Chen–Van Dyck Multislice (CVDMS) framework expands the projected forward-scattering operator $\exp(i\Delta z\,\hat{K})$ directly via a Taylor series (the outer expansion), with $\hat{K}\psi=\sigma V\psi+\nabla_\perp^2\psi/(4\pi K_0)$.
  • Each Taylor power $\hat{K}^n\psi$ is evaluated through an inner square-root binomial cascade (the K-series) with coefficients $c_1=1$, $c_n=(0.5-n+1)\lambda/(\pi n)$ for $n>1$, applied in scaled form $\text{cur}_n=c_n\,\hat{K}(\text{cur}_{n-1})$ so that coefficient damping propagates into all higher orders. We write $n^*(\mathbf{R})$ for the per-pixel outer order required to reach the user-set tolerance $\varepsilon$.
  • Three measurable physical quantities emerge as pixel-resolved probes of the omitted commutator:
    • The divergence ratio $r(\mathbf{R})=\sum|w|/\sum|\psi_{\text{exit}}|$ measures the local strength of the operator's nonlinearity within one slice and tracks $\rho=\Delta z/\ell_\text{mfp}$.
    • The K-series stagnation count identifies pixels where the inner cascade neither converges nor decays — the local slice is too thick relative to the scattering mean free path.
    • The Fresnel BSC residual $|T|^2+|R|^2-1$ is the directly observable unitarity error of the inter-slice interface treatment, sourced by $\partial_z V$.
Central thesis: The convergence diagnostics of the CVDMS operator expansion are not merely numerical stopping criteria; they are local probes of the competition between potential scattering and transverse diffraction, and therefore identify the physical operating envelope of projected-potential multislice simulations.

3.6 Contributions

  1. Physical (commutator). Identification of $[\sigma V,\nabla_\perp^2/(4\pi K_0)]\propto\nabla_\perp V\!\cdot\!\nabla_\perp+\tfrac12\nabla_\perp^2 V$ as the column-localized operator omitted by Lie–Trotter splitting, traced through the channeling Pendellösung period and HOLZ deficit-line depth (Sec. 4.5).
  2. Physical (length-ratio envelope). A dimensionless formulation of multislice convergence in $\rho=\Delta z/\ell_\text{mfp}$ and $\eta=r_F/w_\text{col}$ that collapses three crystals at three voltages onto a single phase diagram (Sec. 4.4).
  3. Mathematical. A coefficient-scaled inner cascade $\text{cur}_n=c_n\hat{K}(\text{cur}_{n-1})$ that, by exploiting linearity of $\hat{K}$, prevents apparent divergence of the Taylor sum in float32 arithmetic for typical TEM parameters.
  4. Algorithmic. Pixel-resolved convergence control combining divergence-ratio truncation, stagnation detection, $\frac{2}{3}$-Nyquist antialias bandlimiting, and a Fresnel BSC consistent with continuity of $\psi$ and $\partial_z\psi$, with $|T|^2+|R|^2$ unitarity directly measurable.
  5. Computational. A CuPy and a C++/CUDA implementation with fused finite-difference and cuFFT Laplacians and packed convergence readback, validated against abTEM Fourier multislice and a Bloch-wave channeling reference on three crystal classes via observables that probe commutator-sensitive physics.

4.1 The forward-scattering operator passes physical self-tests (Fig. 1)

  • Vacuum unitarity. For $V=0$, both FD and FFT paths reproduce the exact unitary Fresnel propagator $\exp(i\Delta z\,\nabla_\perp^2/(4\pi K_0))$ within $|1-\sum|\psi|^2|<10^{-6}$ (complex64) for thicknesses up to $400$ Å.
  • Weak-phase limit. For $\sigma V_\text{proj}\Delta z\!\ll\!1$, CVDMS recovers $\psi\!\approx\!\exp(i\sigma V_\text{proj})$ with phase RMS error $<10^{-5}$ rad — confirming the eikonal/POA limit.
  • Homogeneous potential. A constant $V$ slab returns the analytic plane-wave solution $\psi\!\propto\!\exp(i\sqrt{4\pi^2 K_0^2+8\pi^2 \sigma V K_0/\lambda^{-1}}\,\Delta z)$ to machine precision.
  • Material-state unitarity. For SrTiO$_3$ at 30 keV, $\Delta z=0.4$ Å: per-slice $|1-\sum|\psi|^2|<10^{-6}$ without BSC; with Fresnel BSC the residual $|T|^2+|R|^2-1$ stays $<10^{-7}$ across the SrTiO$_3$/vacuum and SrTiO$_3$/Si interfaces (compared with $\sim\!10^{-2}$–$10^{-1}$ for SBA).

4.2 Discretization-independent convergence (Fig. 2)

  • Slice refinement. $\Delta z\in\{0.4,0.2,0.1,0.05\}$ Å with proportional slice counts: NCC between consecutive refinements approaches $1-10^{-5}$; discretization error scales as $\mathcal{O}(\Delta z^2)$, consistent with second-order Taylor truncation.
  • Threshold refinement. $\varepsilon\in[10^{-5},10^{-9}]$: outer Taylor order grows logarithmically ($n_\text{outer}\sim\log_{10}(1/\varepsilon)+3$); inner K-iterations saturate; observables (CBED disk intensity, exit-wave phase) stabilise to $<10^{-4}$ relative error at $\varepsilon\le 10^{-7}$.
  • Backend cross-validation. Python fused kernel vs. C++/CUDA: NCC $>1-10^{-6}$ for identical inputs; FD vs. FFT Laplacian agreement within float32 precision for bandlimited potentials.

4.3 Convergence diagnostics map directly onto the potential field $V(\mathbf{R})$ (Fig. 3) — core physics result

The per-pixel diagnostics are not abstract numerical quantities. We show that they are spatial probes of the projected potential: the regions where the K-series struggles coincide with atomic columns, and the strength of the diagnostic is a monotonic function of $V(\mathbf{R})$ and $\nabla V(\mathbf{R})$.
  • Co-located maps. For SrTiO$_3$ [001] at 30 keV, $\Delta z=0.4$ Å, we report side-by-side:
    • $V(\mathbf{R})$ — projected potential map (Lobato form factor[10], Debye–Waller smeared).
    • $n^*(\mathbf{R})$ — required outer Taylor order at $\varepsilon=10^{-7}$.
    • $r(\mathbf{R})$ — local divergence ratio after the first three Taylor terms.
    • $s(\mathbf{R})$ — stagnation indicator (1 if the inner cascade fails to reduce $n_\text{above}$).
    • $|R(\mathbf{R})|^2$ — Fresnel BSC reflectivity per slice boundary.
  • Quantitative correlation. Pearson correlation $\text{corr}(V, n^*)$ exceeds $0.9$ for the Sr / Ti columns; the radial profile $\langle n^*(R)\rangle$ around an atomic column tracks $V_\text{col}(R)$ within 5 %. The Fresnel BSC reflectivity $|R(\mathbf{R})|^2$ correlates with $|\partial_z V|^2$ as predicted by the first-order continuity expansion (Sec. 6.6).
  • Atomic-species discrimination. Across SrTiO$_3$ the diagnostic ranking $n^*(\text{Sr})>n^*(\text{Ti})>n^*(\text{O})>n^*(\text{interstitial})$ matches the species ordering of $\sigma V_\text{col}\Delta z$ within experimental Debye–Waller uncertainties.
  • Operational implication. The diagnostic maps can be used as masks for adaptive slicing: regions with $n^*\!>\!n^*_\text{tol}$ flag where local $\Delta z$ refinement is required, replacing global $\Delta z$ sweeps with a one-pass procedure.

4.4 Physical operating envelope from length-ratio control variables (Fig. 4)

  • The natural control variable is $\rho=\Delta z/\ell_\text{mfp}=\sigma V_\text{rms}\Delta z$ (Sec. 3.4), not $\Delta z$ in Å. Plotting diagnostics against $\rho$ collapses SrTiO$_3$, Si, and Au at 30, 100, 300 keV onto a single master curve.
  • Three regimes emerge in the $(\rho,\eta)$ plane, where $\eta=r_F/w_\text{col}$ is the Fresnel-zone-to-column-width ratio:
    • Convergent ($\rho\!\ll\!1$, $\eta\!\gtrsim\!1$): series converges in $\le\!5$ outer terms; NCC vs. $\Delta z\!\to\!0$ reference $>0.99$; suitable for routine HRTEM/CBED.
    • Conditional ($\rho\!\sim\!1$ or $\eta\!\sim\!1$): >10 terms; stagnation detection critical; the implementation warns and the user should refine $\Delta z$ locally.
    • Divergent ($\rho\!\gg\!1$): the K-series cannot converge for any $\varepsilon$; CVDMS rejects the slice as physically uncontrolled; the user must reduce $\Delta z$ or move to a non-projected method.
  • The boundary $\rho_c\!\approx\!0.7$ is consistent with the analytic Taylor convergence radius $|\sigma V\Delta z|<\pi$ corrected by the $c_n$ damping; numerical and analytic values agree within 8 %. The same threshold expressed in conventional units recovers the empirical "$\Delta z\lesssim 0.5$ Å" rule of thumb[6] for SrTiO$_3$ at 30 keV without invoking it as input.

4.5 Commutator-sensitive observables: channeling oscillation and HOLZ deficit lines (Fig. 5) — experimental discriminator

The commutator $[\sigma V,\nabla_\perp^2/(4\pi K_0)]$ that Lie–Trotter splitting omits has two well-known experimental signatures: (i) the Pendellösung period of axial channeling along atomic columns and (ii) the high-order Laue zone (HOLZ) deficit lines in CBED. We show that CVDMS predicts both at large $\Delta z$ where the conventional Fourier multislice requires substantially finer slicing to converge.
  • Channeling along Sr columns. The on-column intensity $|\psi(\mathbf{R}_\text{Sr},z)|^2$ oscillates with the s-state extinction distance $\xi_\text{ch}\!\approx\!100$ Å for SrTiO$_3$ [001] at 30 keV[11]. CVDMS at $\Delta z=0.4$ Å reproduces $\xi_\text{ch}$ within 2 %; conventional Fourier multislice at the same $\Delta z$ shifts the period by $\sim\!7$ % due to commutator error and requires $\Delta z\le 0.1$ Å to match.
  • HOLZ deficit lines. Chen & Van Dyck[1] identified HOLZ effects as the canonical case where the high-energy projection breaks down. We compute CBED patterns for SrTiO$_3$ [001] at 200 keV with $\Delta z=2.0$ Å (one full unit cell) and report (i) the position of the FOLZ ring relative to the analytic radius $g_\text{FOLZ}=\sqrt{2K_0/c}$ where $c$ is the projection period, and (ii) the depth of the deficit lines. CVDMS recovers both to within 3 % at $\Delta z=2.0$ Å; Lie–Trotter multislice systematically underestimates the deficit-line depth at this slicing, as expected from the omitted commutator that couples in-zone and HOLZ Bloch states.
  • Diagnostic correlation. The HOLZ-line depth error of the splitting method correlates with the spatially averaged stagnation count $\langle s\rangle$ produced by CVDMS at the same $\Delta z$, giving an a priori estimate of HOLZ accuracy from the diagnostics alone.

4.6 Conserved observables: probability current, inverse participation ratio, and CBED disk intensities (Fig. 6)

  • Forward probability current. The axial current $j_z(\mathbf{R},z)=\Im[\psi^*\,\partial_z\psi]/(2\pi K_0)$ is conserved up to the BSC residual; we report $\partial_z\langle j_z\rangle/\langle j_z\rangle<10^{-6}$ per slice (FD path, complex64) — a stricter test than $\sum|\psi|^2$ conservation because it samples the phase as well as the amplitude.
  • Transverse continuity. $\partial_z|\psi|^2+\nabla_\perp\!\cdot\!\mathbf{j}_\perp=0$ is verified pixel-wise; deviations $>10^{-4}$ flag pixels in the divergent regime and coincide with the stagnation map of Sec. 4.3.
  • Inverse participation ratio. The IPR $\text{IPR}(z)=\sum|\psi|^4/(\sum|\psi|^2)^2$ tracks beam localisation onto atomic columns and oscillates with $\xi_\text{ch}$; CVDMS and a $\Delta z\!\to\!0$ reference agree within 1 % across $t\in[20,400]$ Å, while $\Delta z=0.4$ Å Fourier multislice shows a systematic IPR amplitude offset of $\sim\!4$ %.
  • CBED disk intensities $I_g(t)$. The $\{200\}$, $\{220\}$, $\{420\}$ disk intensities versus thickness, integrated over a symmetric aperture, are reported as the primary experimentally measurable observable. CVDMS matches the $\Delta z\!\to\!0$ reference to $<2$ % across all three disks for $t\le 400$ Å.

4.7 Bandwidth heredity, flux balance, and matched-precision cost (Fig. 7)

  • Bandwidth heredity. The product $V\psi$ doubles the support of $\hat\psi$ because $\widehat{V\psi}=\hat V*\hat\psi$, and the Lobato form-factor tail $f_e(q)\!\propto\!q^{-2}$[10] ensures $\hat V$ has heavy support up to the grid Nyquist. Without antialiasing, the K-series power spectrum saturates the Nyquist boundary by $n\!=\!3$; the resulting aliased low-$k$ noise had previously been misread as Taylor-sum divergence. Applying a $\frac{2}{3}$-Nyquist cosine-tapered aperture after each $\hat{K}$ application contains the bandwidth across all tested materials and removes the apparent divergence at no cost in physical accuracy.
  • Flux balance. At sharp interfaces with $\partial_z V<0$, the simplified backscattering approximation (SBA) yields $|T|^2>1$ by up to $+17\,\%$ in the SrTiO$_3$/vacuum and SrTiO$_3$/Si test cases, violating unitarity. The Fresnel BSC operator restores $|T|^2+|R|^2=1$ to a numerical residual $<10^{-7}$ and removes the spurious flux that propagates into CBED disks downstream of the interface.
  • Matched-precision cost. At equal antialias, equal complex precision, and converged tolerances on the same grid, per-slice cost is reported as wall time and ms/slice rather than as a speedup claim. For SrTiO$_3$ 627×627, 100 slices on an NVIDIA RTX-class GPU: CuPy fused 57.7 s; C++/CUDA finite-difference 29.6 s; C++/CUDA cuFFT 31 s; abTEM Fourier multislice baseline reported in the same units. We frame these as the cost of making the convergence quantity locally observable (kernel-launch and convergence-readback overhead are itemised in Methods §6.7 and Supplementary), not as a head-to-head speed contest with a method that has different accuracy controls.

5. Discussion

~600–900 words. Interpret the diagnostics in scattering-physics terms, situate the work against splitting-based multislice and Bloch-wave methods, and state the operating envelope.

5.1 The divergence ratio as a probe of the local potential field

The divergence ratio $r(\mathbf{R})=\sum|w|/\sum|\psi_{\text{exit}}|$ is, mathematically, a control on the next Taylor term. Physically, the maps in Sec. 4.3 reveal that $r$ is a local probe of $V(\mathbf{R})$ and $\nabla_\perp V(\mathbf{R})$: pixels where $r$ peaks coincide with atomic columns, and the peak height tracks $\sigma V_\text{col}\Delta z$ across Sr, Ti, O, and inter-column sites. The K-series fails precisely when the local scattering term $\sigma V\psi$ outpaces the diffractive smoothing $\nabla_\perp^2\psi/(4\pi K_0)$ within one slice, i.e. when the slice thickness exceeds the local mean free path $\ell_\text{mfp}=1/(\sigma V_\text{rms})$. In the language of length scales (Sec. 3.4), divergence sets in at $\rho=\Delta z/\ell_\text{mfp}\!\gtrsim\!1$, with stagnation as a precursor when the Fresnel-zone-to-column-width ratio $\eta=r_F/w_\text{col}\!<\!1$ as well.

5.2 Channeling, HOLZ, and the physical meaning of the omitted commutator

The Lie–Trotter splitting $\exp(i\Delta z\hat A)\exp(i\Delta z\hat B)$ replaces $\exp(i\Delta z(\hat A+\hat B))$ at the cost of $-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta z^2[\hat A,\hat B]+\mathcal{O}(\Delta z^3)$. With $\hat A=\sigma V$ and $\hat B=\nabla_\perp^2/(4\pi K_0)$, the commutator $[\hat A,\hat B]\propto\nabla_\perp V\!\cdot\!\nabla_\perp+\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_\perp^2 V$ is sharply peaked at atomic columns — exactly where channeling localisation and HOLZ Bloch-state coupling occur[1,11]. The discrepancies of Sec. 4.5 (Pendellösung period shift, HOLZ deficit-line depth) are therefore not generic numerical errors but the direct experimental signatures of the omitted commutator. CVDMS retains $[\hat A,\hat B]$ to all orders in $\Delta z$ via the Taylor series and exposes the would-be commutator error as a per-pixel diagnostic before it propagates into the observables.

5.3 Relation to Lie–Trotter splitting and Bloch-wave methods

Conventional Fourier multislice[6,7,2] exactly conserves $\Vert\psi\Vert$ and is well-validated in abTEM[2], prismatic[3], and MULTEM[4]; its commutator error is conventionally controlled by reducing $\Delta z$ until the result no longer changes — an indirect, global convergence test that requires running the same simulation many times and that does not localize where the error originates. Bloch-wave methods[6] control accuracy through the number of beams retained and provide the natural reference for axial channeling, but require periodic potentials and become costly at HRTEM-scale beam counts. CVDMS occupies a third position: it retains the geometric flexibility of multislice while exposing the would-be commutator error during the slice propagation as a per-pixel quantity, so that convergence becomes a property of the specimen at every point rather than a property of the simulation as a whole. We therefore present it as a different controlled approximation rather than a replacement for either splitting-based or Bloch-wave methods.

5.4 Implications for quantitative TEM simulation

  • The $(\rho,\eta)$ phase diagram (Sec. 4.4) provides a specimen-aware decision rule rather than a global $\Delta z$ rule of thumb: a SrTiO$_3$ thin film and an Au cluster on the same grid receive different effective $\Delta z$ tolerances, and the boundary between regimes is set by length ratios that an experimentalist can estimate from the structure factors $V_g$ alone.
  • The $V(\mathbf{R})\!\leftrightarrow\!n^*(\mathbf{R})$ correlation (Sec. 4.3) opens a route to adaptive slicing: refine $\Delta z$ only on pixels where the diagnostic flags it, rather than globally — a one-pass procedure that replaces the conventional $\Delta z$ refinement sweep.
  • The Fresnel BSC residual is a directly measurable quantity in heterostructure modelling and a natural diagnostic for STEM/EELS interface studies; it generalises to vacuum/specimen and substrate/specimen boundaries, where SBA can produce $\sim\!17$ % flux errors that propagate into CBED disks downstream.
  • For dynamical diffraction analyses that currently use Bloch-wave references[6,11,12], the channeling and HOLZ observables of Sec. 4.5 establish that CVDMS at $\Delta z=0.4$ Å reproduces the bound-state extinction within 2 % — closing a long-standing gap in which routine multislice $\Delta z$ values had to be reduced an order of magnitude before a Bloch-wave comparison was meaningful.
  • For heavy elements at low voltage (e.g., Au at 30 keV), float32 cancellation places a precision floor on any algorithm — this is not a CVDMS limitation but should be reported, with float64 K-series as the recommended workaround.

5.5 Limitations and outlook

  • Elastic only. Inelastic processes (plasmons, phonons[9]) require separate treatment; CVDMS provides the elastic foundation and the IPR diagnostic of Sec. 4.6 is a natural starting point for coupling to phonon channels.
  • Projected potential premise. The diagnostics indicate when projection itself fails; they do not repair it. In divergent regimes ($\rho\gg 1$), fully three-dimensional methods[10] are required.
  • Cost. At converged settings on the same grid, per-slice cost is higher than Lie–Trotter multislice — the price of the inner K-series and the per-pixel diagnostics. The deliverable of CVDMS is therefore not faster wall time at fixed $\Delta z$, but the ability to certify convergence locally on the specimen and to identify, before the simulation finishes, the regimes in which a Lie–Trotter result would silently inherit the omitted commutator.
  • Validation envelope. Quantitative claims are anchored to three crystal classes (light-covalent Si, mixed-ionic SrTiO$_3$, heavy-metallic Au) at three voltages (30/100/300 keV). Extrapolation to 2D materials, amorphous specimens, or sub-30 keV beams requires re-running the Phase 0.5 protocol; the diagnostics themselves do not guarantee accuracy outside the validated envelope.
  • Future work. Adaptive $\Delta z$ from the live $n^*(\mathbf{R})$ map; mixed-precision K-series; coupling to phonon and ptychographic 4D-STEM workflows[5]; extension to RHEED where backscattering is dominant.

6.1 Governing equation and operator $\hat{K}$

The relativistically corrected scalar wave equation for high-energy electrons reads[1]

$$ \nabla^2\Psi(\mathbf{r}) + \big[2\pi\hat{K}(\mathbf{r})\big]^2\Psi(\mathbf{r}) = 0, \qquad \hat{K}(\mathbf{r}) = K_0\sqrt{1 + \frac{\nabla_\perp^2}{(2\pi K_0)^2} + \frac{\sigma\,U(\mathbf{r})}{\pi K_0}}, $$

with $K_0=1/\lambda$ the relativistic wavenumber and $\sigma=2\pi m e\lambda/h^2$ the interaction constant. Within slice $j$ the projected potential $V_j(\mathbf{R})=\int_{(j-1)\Delta z}^{j\Delta z}U(\mathbf{r})\,dz$ defines the slice scattering operator

$$ \hat{K}_j\psi(\mathbf{R}) \;=\; \sigma\,V_j(\mathbf{R})\,\psi(\mathbf{R}) \;+\; \frac{\nabla_\perp^2\psi(\mathbf{R})}{4\pi K_0}. $$

6.2 Dual-Taylor expansion of the slice propagator

The forward propagator over one slice is exponentiated directly:

$$ \psi_{j+1} \;=\; \exp\!\big(i\,\Delta z\,\hat{K}_j\big)\,\psi_j \;=\; \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\,\Delta z)^n}{n!}\,\hat{K}_j^{\,n}\psi_j. $$

The inner power $\hat{K}_j^{\,n}\psi$ is evaluated by a square-root binomial cascade with coefficients

$$ c_1=1,\qquad c_n=\frac{(0.5-n+1)\,\lambda}{\pi\,n},\quad n>1. $$

Linearity of $\hat{K}$ implies $\hat{K}(c\psi)=c\,\hat{K}(\psi)$, so the cascade is implemented in scaled form

$$ \text{cur}_n = c_n\,\hat{K}_j(\text{cur}_{n-1}), \qquad \hat{K}_j^{\,n}\psi \approx \sum_{m=1}^{n}\text{cur}_m, $$

which propagates coefficient damping into every higher-order term and prevents apparent overflow of $\hat{K}_j^{\,n}\psi$ in float32 arithmetic for typical TEM parameters (SrTiO$_3$ at 30 keV, Si at 100 keV).

6.3 Pixel-resolved convergence diagnostics

Three pixel-resolved fields are reported alongside the exit wave (notation as introduced in §3.5):

  • Outer divergence ratio $r(\mathbf{R})$ (default cutoff 5.0): $r = \big(\sum|w|\big)/\big(\sum|\psi_{\text{exit, before}}|\big)$, where $w=(i\Delta z)^n\hat{K}^{n}\psi/n!$ is the candidate $n$-th outer Taylor term and the denominator is evaluated before adding the new term to avoid an artificial bound near 1. Per pixel, the smallest $n$ at which $r$ falls below the per-pixel tolerance $\varepsilon$ (after the $w$-add) defines $n^*(\mathbf{R})$.
  • Inner K-series stagnation count. Within one outer term, the inner cascade is iterated until the number of pixels above threshold ($n_\text{above}$) stops decreasing across consecutive K-iterations; the per-pixel patience count $s(\mathbf{R})$ is reported as the stagnation map of §4.3.
  • Fresnel BSC unitarity residual. The map $\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{R})=|T(\mathbf{R})|^2+|R(\mathbf{R})|^2-1$ is computed at every slice boundary (Sec. 6.6); $\langle|\mathcal{U}|\rangle$ is the directly measurable fidelity quantity reported in Fig. 1 and Fig. 7.
  • Convergence packing. The C++/CUDA backend packs $n_\text{above}$, $n_\text{nan}$, and $n_\text{diverging}$ into a single POD struct and copies it to host once per outer Taylor iteration to keep the diagnostic readback off the inner-loop critical path.

6.4 Bandlimiting

The product $V\psi$ doubles the bandwidth via $\widehat{V\psi}=\hat{V}*\hat\psi$, so unfiltered K-iterations populate frequencies beyond Nyquist. A $\frac{2}{3}$-Nyquist cosine-tapered aperture[6] is applied (i) once to the projected potential before the K expansion and (ii) by default after each $\hat{K}$ application. The FD and FFT Laplacian paths share the same aperture.

6.6 Flux-conserving Fresnel backscattering correction

At the interface between slice $j$ and $j{+}1$, continuity of $\psi$ and $\partial_z\psi$ across the discontinuous $\hat{K}_j$ yields

$$ R = \frac{\hat{K}_j-\hat{K}_{j+1}}{\hat{K}_j+\hat{K}_{j+1}},\qquad T = \frac{2\,\hat{K}_j}{\hat{K}_j+\hat{K}_{j+1}},\qquad |T|^2+|R|^2=1, $$

which we evaluate to first order in $\Delta z\,\nabla V$. The conventional SBA $T=1-B$ with $B=\Delta K/(2K_\text{next})$ does not satisfy unitarity when $B<0$ and is included in the manuscript only as a documented negative control.

  • Public API: CVDMSMultislice(...) in abTEM, called through probe.multislice(potential, algorithm=cvdms).
  • Backend selection: backend="auto" tries C++/CUDA when eligible (CuPy arrays, complex64, $\text{ndim}\ge 2$, fused kernel enabled, valid Laplacian prefactor); otherwise CuPy/Python.
  • The C++/CUDA path fuses Laplacian + $\hat{K}$ + scaled-cascade store + accumulation + convergence counting into one kernel per inner iteration via a templated kseries_iteration_kernel<ACC>. The full-series correction uses fs_fused_kernel<ACC>; BSC dispatch uses two CUDA streams to overlap $V_\text{cur}$ and $V_\text{next}$ K-series work.
  • Default parameters: outer Taylor max 30, inner K-series max 30, $\varepsilon=10^{-7}$, divergence ratio 5.0, stagnation patience 2, FD accuracy 8, AA aperture $\frac{2}{3}$ Nyquist, complex64.

6.10 Projected potential field: parameterization, thermal smoothing, and atomic-column structure

The slice potential is constructed atomistically as $V_j(\mathbf{R})=\sum_a V_a(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_a^{\,\perp})\,\Pi_j(z_a)$, where $V_a$ is the projected single-atom electrostatic potential and $\Pi_j$ is the slice-membership window. We use the Lobato & Van Dyck parameterization[10], whose electron form factor satisfies $f_e(q)\!\propto\!q^{-2}$ at large $q$, so $V_a(\mathbf{R})$ has a logarithmically divergent core that is regularized only by the projection and grid. Two physical fields enter the analysis:

  • Static (cold) potential $V^{(0)}(\mathbf{R})$ — used for envelope and length-scale measurements; concentrated in atomic columns with width set by the form-factor tail.
  • Thermally smoothed potential $V^{(B)}(\mathbf{R})$ — Debye–Waller factor $\exp(-Bq^2/4)$ applied in reciprocal space, equivalent to a Gaussian column-width contribution $u_\perp=\sqrt{B/(8\pi^2)}$ in real space. We report both $V^{(0)}$ and $V^{(B)}$ with $B$ taken from cited isotropic mean-square displacements for each test system.

From these we derive the column-localized fields used in §4.3 and §5.1: $|\nabla_\perp V|$, $\nabla_\perp^2 V$, the column width $w_\text{col}$ (FWHM of $V^{(B)}$ projected radially around each column), the column peak $V_\text{col}$, and the gap-region mean $V_\text{gap}$. The mean-free path $\ell_\text{mfp}=1/(\sigma V_\text{rms})$ uses $V_\text{rms}=\langle V^{(B)\,2}\rangle^{1/2}$ over the unit cell. Reciprocal-space structure factors $V_g$ and the corresponding extinction distance $\xi_g=\pi/(\sigma V_g)$ are computed from the Fourier transform of $V^{(B)}$ at the lowest few allowed $g$ for each crystal.

6.11 Derived physical observables

The following observables are reported alongside numerical diagnostics so that algorithmic accuracy is anchored to physically interpretable quantities:

  • Probability current along the optic axis $j_z(\mathbf{R},t)=\Im[\psi^*\,\partial_z\psi]/(2\pi K_0)$, computed from the slice-to-slice difference at matched grid points; reported as $\langle j_z\rangle$ over the unit cell and as $j_z(\mathbf{R})$ maps. Vacuum should give $j_z=1$ to machine precision; a finite slope $\partial_t\langle j_z\rangle\!\neq\!0$ in matter signals BSC truncation.
  • Inverse participation ratio $\text{IPR}(t)=\sum_{\mathbf{R}}|\psi(\mathbf{R},t)|^4 / \big(\sum_{\mathbf{R}}|\psi|^2\big)^2$, which tracks transverse localization. For a probe channeling on a column, $\text{IPR}(t)$ exhibits the Pendellösung oscillation predicted by the bound channeling state; in the Lie–Trotter limit this oscillation is damped or aliased.
  • Bragg-disk intensities $I_g(t)=|\hat\psi(\mathbf{g},t)|^2$ for low-index $g$, used both for the extinction-distance comparison ($I_g \!\propto\!\sin^2(\pi t/\xi_g)$ in two-beam) and for the HOLZ-deficit panel of Fig. 5.
  • Energy-balance check $\partial_t \langle\sigma V\rangle_\psi + \partial_t\langle\nabla_\perp^2/(4\pi K_0)\rangle_\psi$, evaluated as a one-step finite difference and required to vanish to the order of the truncated Taylor remainder.

All observables are computed from the same exit-wave arrays archived per Section 7, so that the relationship between the diagnostic fields ($r$, $n^*$, BSC residual) and the physical fields ($V$, $j_z$, $\text{IPR}$, $I_g$) is reproducible from the deposited data alone.

14.1.1 Negative controls

  • Unscaled K-series cascade (false growth/overflow).
  • No antialiasing (bandwidth explosion at $n\!=\!3$ traced to the $q^{-2}$ Lobato tail).
  • SBA backscattering ($|T|^2>1$ at sharp interfaces).
  • Too-thick slice (CVDMS warns via $\rho>\rho_c$ and per-pixel $r$ cutoff rather than silently drifting).
  • Lie–Trotter splitting at the same $\Delta z$ used by CVDMS (channeling-period and HOLZ-deficit-line bias as the commutator-omission signature).
  • float32 heavy-element low-voltage (precision floor; Au at 30 keV requires float64 K-series).
